Illustrative case study

Resource Guarding & Food Aggression

The concern: A dog stiffens, freezes and growls when someone approaches while eating. The owner is worried the behaviour may escalate into a bite.

A short clip is not enough to diagnose the cause with certainty. My assessment looks at the whole sequence: what happens before the response, the dog's body language, the distance to the person and how the person reacts.

Key moments to look for

1. Before the approach: Is the dog relaxed and eating normally, or already tense and watchful?

2. As the person approaches: Does the dog pause eating, lower its head over the bowl, turn away, show the whites of its eyes or become still?

3. When distance closes: Does the dog growl, bare teeth or snap? These are important warnings, not behaviour to punish.

4. After the person moves away: Does the dog relax again? That change helps identify the distance and situation associated with the response.

What I observed

In this illustrative sequence, the dog's stillness and tension precede the growl. The growl is not the beginning of the problem; it is a later warning that the dog feels uncomfortable about someone approaching a valued resource.

My assessment

The pattern is consistent with resource guarding. Possible contributors include fear of losing food, previous experiences and stress. A video can show the pattern but cannot establish a definitive diagnosis or rule out pain.

What to avoid

Do not take the bowl away to test the dog, reach into the food, punish growling, corner the dog or force repeated approaches. These actions can increase risk and may suppress warning signals.

Immediate safety

Feed the dog in a quiet, separate area where children and visitors cannot approach. Let the dog finish undisturbed. Use barriers and routines to prevent confrontations while a tailored plan is developed.

Practical training plan

Change the association, not just the reaction.

The objective is for the dog to feel safer when people are nearby, rather than learning that warnings are punished. Progress should be gradual and based on relaxed body language, never on pushing through a growl.

Staged approach

Stage 1 — Management: Establish a predictable feeding location and prevent interruptions. Record the situations and distances at which tension appears without deliberately provoking it.

Stage 2 — Positive association: With professional guidance where aggression is present, work at a distance where the dog remains comfortable. A person appearing can predict an extra high-value treat delivered safely, without approaching the bowl or requiring the dog to surrender food.

Stage 3 — Build alternative skills: Away from meals, teach voluntary movement away from items, recall and low-value exchanges using rewards. Do not practise trades with guarded food or high-value items when there is a bite risk.

Stage 4 — Review and adjust: Only reduce distance or increase difficulty when the dog consistently remains loose and comfortable. If tension returns, increase distance and reassess.

What progress looks like

More relaxed eating, less vigilance when people are at a safe distance, and an ability to disengage voluntarily in separate training exercises. Progress is measured in comfort and predictability, not simply the absence of growling.

Further considerations

Sudden onset or worsening guarding warrants a veterinary check for pain or illness. Any history of bites, snapping at people or children in the home calls for an individual risk assessment and support from a suitably qualified, reward-based behaviour professional. Video analysis alone is not a substitute for hands-on help in a high-risk case.
